# From Offer to the First 90 Days

> Everything else in this repo stops at "you got the offer". This is what happens next — and the
> first 90 days determine whether the job you won is the job you keep and enjoy.
>
> It also closes a loop: the reason to be good at
> [reading unfamiliar code](../phase-1-core-programming/06-reading-unfamiliar-code.md) is that
> you are about to do it full-time for a month.

## In 60 seconds

1. **An offer is a starting position, not a verdict.** Companies expect negotiation and budget
   for it. Not negotiating leaves money on the table roughly every time.
2. **Negotiate the whole package, not just base.** Signing bonus, equipment, learning budget,
   start date and title are often easier to move than salary — and title compounds for your next
   role.
3. **Get everything in writing before you resign.** Verbal offers are withdrawn more often than
   people believe, and you cannot un-resign.
4. **Your first 30 days are for learning, not shipping.** The strongest new hires spend week one
   understanding one path end to end and asking sharp questions — not opening a large PR.
5. **Ship something small in week one anyway.** Not for impact — to exercise the whole path from
   ticket to production and find out where it is broken.
6. **Write down everything confusing.** Your beginner's confusion is a genuinely perishable
   asset, and the notes become the onboarding doc that did not exist.

**The most common first-90-days mistake:** arriving with strong opinions and applying them
immediately. "At my last company we did X" in week two costs credibility that takes months to
rebuild. Earn the right first.

## Key terms in this guide

| Term | Plain meaning |
|---|---|
| **Base salary** | The fixed annual amount |
| **Total compensation (TC)** | Base + bonus + equity + benefits |
| **Signing bonus** | One-off payment on joining. Often the easiest thing to move |
| **Equity / options** | A share of the company. Meaningless without the details below |
| **Vesting** | Earning equity over time, usually 4 years with a 1-year cliff |
| **Cliff** | Nothing vests until you pass it. Leave at 11 months, get nothing |
| **Strike price** | What you pay to exercise an option |
| **Notice period** | How long you must work after resigning |
| **Counter-offer** | Your current employer trying to keep you |
| **Probation** | An initial period with shorter notice on both sides |
| **Onboarding** | Everything that gets you productive |
| **Ramp time** | How long until you are contributing at full rate |
| **30/60/90 plan** | A written plan for your first three months |

## 1. Evaluating the offer

**Compare total compensation, not base salary** — and for an international remote role, compare
what actually reaches you.

```
   base salary
 + annual bonus (and is it guaranteed or discretionary?)
 + equity, per year of vesting
 + employer pension/provident contribution
 + health insurance value
 + equipment and home-office budget
 + learning budget
 ─────────────────────────────
 = total compensation

 then subtract, for international contracts:
   - your own tax handling (contractor vs employee)
   - currency conversion and transfer fees
   - unpaid leave, if there is no paid allowance
```

**Contractor vs employee matters a lot** for a BD-based engineer working with a foreign company.
A "higher" contractor rate can be lower after you handle your own tax, have no paid leave, and
no notice protection. Ask which arrangement it is, explicitly, before comparing numbers.

**The non-monetary factors that determine whether you stay:**

| Factor | Why it matters more than people weight it |
|---|---|
| **The manager** | The single biggest predictor of whether you enjoy the job |
| **On-call load** | Ask for the number. You got it in [the reverse interview](10-reverse-interview-bank.md) |
| **Overlap hours required** | A hard 6-hour EU overlap from GMT+6 is a lifestyle decision |
| **Growth path** | Who was promoted recently, and for what |
| **What you'd work on** | The first problem they named for you |
| **Written culture** | From GMT+6, a meeting-driven company will route around you |

## 2. Negotiating

**Companies expect it and have budgeted for it.** Declining to negotiate is not politeness; it
is leaving money that was allocated to you.

**The rules:**

**1. Never accept on the call.** Always:
> "Thank you, I'm really pleased. Could you send the details in writing? I'd like to review the
> full package and come back to you within a couple of days."

**2. Negotiate once, comprehensively.** Going back three times is exhausting for them and weakens
you. Put everything in one message.

**3. Anchor on market value and scope, not on need.** "I have expenses" is not an argument.
"Comparable senior backend roles with this scope are in X range" is.

**4. Ask for a specific number**, not "more".

**The message that works:**

```
Thank you — I'm excited about the role and the team.

I'd like to discuss the compensation before I accept. Based on my research
for senior backend roles at this scope, and given the event-driven and
real-time experience I'd be bringing at 41M-user scale, I was hoping for
[specific number].

Is there flexibility there? If base is fixed, I'd also be glad to discuss
a signing bonus or an earlier review date.
```

**Note the last line.** It gives them a way to say yes when base is genuinely constrained by
band — and signing bonuses come from a different budget, which is why they move more easily.

**What is negotiable, roughly in order of ease:**

| Item | Flexibility |
|---|---|
| Signing bonus | **High** — different budget, one-off |
| Start date | High |
| Equipment / home-office budget | High |
| Learning and conference budget | High |
| Title | **Medium — and it compounds into your next role** |
| Extra leave | Medium |
| Base salary | Medium — often band-constrained |
| Equity | Varies wildly |
| Remote arrangement | Usually already fixed |

**Title is underrated.** `Senior` versus `Lead` on your next CV affects the level you are
screened at for years. If base is immovable, push here.

**If they refuse everything**, that is information — about budget, and about how they treat
requests. Accepting gracefully is fine. Do not manufacture a competing offer that does not
exist; it collapses badly and the market is small.

## 3. Equity, if it is offered

Common in startups, and frequently misunderstood. **A percentage means nothing on its own.**

**Ask these, and expect answers:**

| Question | Why |
|---|---|
| How many shares, and what is the total outstanding? | "0.1%" is meaningless without the denominator |
| What was the last valuation, and when? | An 18-month-old valuation may be fiction |
| Strike price? | What you pay to exercise |
| Vesting schedule and cliff? | Standard: 4 years, 1-year cliff |
| Exercise window after leaving? | Often 90 days — you may have to buy or lose them |
| Options or RSUs? | Different tax treatment entirely |
| Any liquidation preference above me? | Investors may be paid first, leaving common shares worth nothing |

**How to weight it:** for a private company, treat equity as a lottery ticket with a real but
unknowable value. **Do not accept below-market cash for equity unless you can afford the
downside.** Most startup equity ends up worth zero — that is the base rate, not pessimism.

For a BD-based engineer, add: how would you actually exercise and sell across borders, and what
does that cost in tax and transfer? Sometimes the honest answer is that the equity is
impractical, and that should change how you value it.

## 4. Before you resign

**Get the signed contract first.** Not an email saying "we'd love to have you" — the actual
document, with:

- [ ] Title, start date, and reporting line
- [ ] Salary and payment currency and schedule
- [ ] Bonus terms, and whether they are guaranteed
- [ ] Equity details in writing
- [ ] Notice period and probation terms
- [ ] Leave allowance
- [ ] Contractor vs employee status stated explicitly
- [ ] Any background check completed or explicitly waived

**Then, and only then, resign.** Offers are withdrawn — funding changes, headcount freezes,
background checks stall. You cannot un-resign, and the gap between resigning and a withdrawn
offer is the worst position in this entire document.

## 5. Resigning well

**Your industry is smaller than it looks.** Today's manager is a future reference or a future
colleague, and Dhaka's backend community is not large.

| Do | Don't |
|---|---|
| Tell your manager first, directly, before anyone else | Let them hear it from someone else |
| Give the notice in your contract | Vanish, or under-serve the notice |
| Write handover docs — the ones you wished existed | Leave undocumented knowledge in your head |
| Offer to be available for questions briefly | Promise indefinite support |
| Stay professional in the exit interview | Use it to settle scores |
| Say thank you, and mean it | Burn it down on the way out |

**On counter-offers:** if you leave for reasons other than money, a raise does not fix them.
Statistically, most people who accept counter-offers leave within a year anyway — and having
signalled you were looking, you are now the first name in a downturn. Accept one only if the
*specific* thing that made you leave has genuinely changed.

**The handover doc is the best last impression available.** A written map of what you owned, its
failure modes and its landmines is remembered long after your notice period.

## 6. Days 1–30: learn

**Goal: build an accurate model of the system, the team and the domain. Not to ship features.**

| Week | Focus |
|---|---|
| **1** | Environment running. Trace one behaviour end to end. Meet everyone. **Ship one tiny thing** |
| **2** | Trace two more paths. Read the tests. Start reviewing PRs — you learn fastest as a reviewer |
| **3** | Take a real, small ticket. Ask about anything confusing while asking is still free |
| **4** | Write down the model you have built. Share it |

**Ship something in week one, deliberately small.** A copy fix, a log line, a flaky test. The
point is not impact — it is exercising **the whole path**: local setup, branch, review, CI,
deploy. You will discover where that path is broken, and that is genuinely useful information
that only a new person can gather.

**Use the tracing method** from
[06-reading-unfamiliar-code](../phase-1-core-programming/06-reading-unfamiliar-code.md).
It is the same skill, and the same `NOTES.md`.

**Ask questions while they are free.** There is a window — roughly the first month — where "why
is this like this?" costs you nothing. After that it starts to look like you should already
know. **Front-load your confusion.**

**Keep a "things that confused me" list.** Your beginner's perspective disappears in about six
weeks and never returns. Turn it into the onboarding doc that did not exist, and you have made a
visible contribution before writing much code.

**What not to do in month one:**
- Propose a large refactor
- Say "at my last company we did X"
- Rewrite anything you do not fully understand
- Open a 900-line PR

## 7. Days 31–60: contribute

**Goal: deliver real work independently, at the team's normal quality.**

- Take tickets end to end without hand-holding
- Review others' PRs substantively — this is where you start being seen as senior
- Be on call, or shadow it. **Nothing teaches a system faster than being paged by it**
- Fix one thing that annoyed you in month one — small, well-scoped
- Write one document: an ADR, a design doc, or the onboarding notes

**By day 60 you should be able to answer:** where does a change like this go, who needs to know,
what breaks if it is wrong, and how would I find out.

**If you are not there by day 60, say so before someone else notices.** "I'm slower on X than
I'd like — can we pair on it?" is a strength signal. Silently struggling is the failure mode.

## 8. Days 61–90: own

**Goal: own something, and start influencing how work is done.**

- Take ownership of a service, a subsystem or an area
- Lead one project end to end, including the write-up
- Propose one improvement **with evidence** — now you have earned the standing
- Ask for feedback explicitly: "what would you want to see more of from me?"

**The 90-day review.** Many companies have one formally; if yours does not, ask for it. Come
with your own list: what you shipped, what you learned, where you need support.

**By day 90 you should be:** the person someone asks about at least one part of the system. That
is the actual definition of ramped.

## 9. Remote-specific, from GMT+6

Working with a European or US team from Dhaka adds a layer that nobody briefs you on.

| Challenge | What to do |
|---|---|
| **You miss the informal conversation** | Ask for decisions to be summarised in writing. Say it early, as a preference, not a complaint |
| **Being forgotten in planning** | Be visible in writing: post updates, comment on docs, be the person whose thinking is legible |
| **Your overlap window is precious** | Protect it for synchronous things only. Do deep work outside it |
| **Timezone drift into your evening** | Set a boundary in month one, before a norm forms. It is nearly impossible to reset later |
| **Fewer visible wins** | Write more. A short weekly update is disproportionately effective when you are not in the room |
| **Isolation** | Book optional 1:1s with two or three colleagues. Relationships do not form by accident remotely |

**The one that matters most:** ask in week one how decisions get made. If the honest answer is
"in the standup", you have a structural problem and it is far cheaper to raise it in week one
than in month six.

**Your written English is now doing the work your presence would.** This is the payoff for the
artifacts in
[../resume/02-linkedin-and-profiles.md](../resume/02-linkedin-and-profiles.md#8-the-english-problem-and-four-cheap-fixes)
— the habit built while job hunting is the habit that makes remote work well.

## 10. The mistakes that cost people the job

| Mistake | Why it costs you |
|---|---|
| **Big opinions in month one** | You do not yet know why things are as they are. Chesterton's Fence |
| **Not asking questions** | Reads as either disengaged or hiding confusion. Both are worse than asking |
| **Only asking questions** | At senior level, some independent progress is expected. Time-box before escalating |
| **A giant first PR** | Unreviewable, and it signals poor scoping judgement |
| **Not shipping anything for a month** | Even at senior level, visible progress matters early |
| **Ignoring the onboarding buddy** | They are the cheapest source of context you will ever have |
| **Skipping the boring docs** | The runbook and the incident history are where the real system is described |
| **Staying silent when struggling** | The only unrecoverable one. Say it at day 45, not day 85 |

**The single best habit for the first 90 days:** end each week with three lines — what I learned,
what I shipped, what I am still confused about. It takes five minutes, it makes your 90-day
review write itself, and the third line is the one that keeps you honest.
