# The Interview Loop — What the Whole Process Looks Like

> Every other guide in this repo prepares you for **one round**. This one is the map: what the
> full process is, in what order, who is in the room, and what each stage is actually filtering
> for.
>
> Knowing the shape removes a surprising amount of anxiety, and it lets you prepare in the order
> the funnel actually cuts.

## In 60 seconds

1. **A typical senior loop is 4–6 stages over 3–6 weeks.** Recruiter screen → technical screen →
   take-home or online assessment → system design → behavioural / bar raiser → offer.
2. **Each stage filters for something different.** Preparing evenly across them is inefficient —
   prepare in proportion to where you are actually being cut.
3. **The two biggest cuts for your profile are the earliest ones:** the automated assessment and
   the technical screen. That is good news, because early-funnel problems are the most trainable.
4. **You can and should ask for the process map up front.** "What does the interview process
   look like end to end?" is a normal recruiter-screen question and it lets you prepare
   specifically.
5. **Different company types run genuinely different loops.** A BD product company, a European
   startup, a US enterprise and a marketplace like Toptal or Proxify are four different games.
6. **The whole thing takes weeks, and several run in parallel.** Which is why the pipeline
   tracker exists — by week three you will not remember who is at which stage.

**The most common process mistake:** treating every stage as equally weighted, and so spending
week one on system design when the online assessment is what is actually removing you.

## Key terms in this guide

| Term | Plain meaning |
|---|---|
| **Loop** | The full sequence of interviews for one role |
| **Recruiter screen** | 20–30 min call. Logistics, salary range, basic fit |
| **Technical screen** | 45–60 min. Usually live coding, sometimes with a hiring manager |
| **OA** | Online Assessment — the automated test |
| **Take-home** | An assignment done alone, then discussed |
| **Onsite / final loop** | Several back-to-back rounds, now usually remote |
| **System design round** | 45–60 min architecture discussion |
| **Behavioural round** | Past-behaviour questions, usually STAR-shaped |
| **Bar raiser** | An interviewer from outside the team, checking the standard is held |
| **Hiring manager round** | Your prospective boss. Often the deciding vote |
| **Debrief** | Interviewers meet and compare notes. Where the decision is made |
| **Offer stage** | Compensation discussion, references, background check |
| **Rejection at stage N** | Useful data. Three at the same stage is a pattern |

## 1. The standard loop

```
   ①  Recruiter screen              20-30 min   · logistics, range, level
              ↓
   ②  Online assessment  OR  Take-home    60-90 min / 4-8 hrs
              ↓                            ← the biggest single cut
   ③  Technical screen              45-60 min   · live coding
              ↓
   ┌──────── final loop, often one day ────────┐
   │  ④  System design               45-60 min │  ← decides senior vs lead
   │  ⑤  Behavioural / hiring manager 45 min   │
   │  ⑥  Bar raiser or peer round     45 min   │
   └───────────────────────────────────────────┘
              ↓
       debrief → offer → negotiation → references
```

**Roughly 3–6 weeks end to end**, with the gaps between stages usually longer than the stages.

**Variations you will meet:**
- Some companies put the take-home *before* any human contact. Others use it instead of the
  final technical round.
- Startups often collapse ③ and ④ into one longer session.
- Marketplaces (Toptal, Proxify, Turing) front-load everything: a heavy assessment, then an
  English/communication check, then you are in a pool and each *client* interviews you again.

## 2. What each stage is actually filtering for

This is the part worth internalising, because it tells you what to optimise.

| Stage | Ostensibly | Actually filtering for | Your prep |
|---|---|---|---|
| **① Recruiter screen** | "Tell me about yourself" | Level, salary alignment, communication, visa/timezone | [Resume summary](../resume/01-master-resume.md#5-summary-lines-for-different-job-postings), spoken 90-second intro |
| **② OA / take-home** | Can you code? | **Can you produce under a clock, unsupervised** | [Phase 0 banks](README.md), [take-home guide](09-take-home-assignments.md) |
| **③ Technical screen** | Can you code? | Can you *think out loud* while coding | [Live coding](11-live-coding-and-pairing.md) |
| **④ System design** | Can you architect? | **Can you have a structured technical conversation under ambiguity** | [Phase 5](../phase-5-system-design/01-system-design-fundamentals.md) |
| **⑤ Behavioural** | Culture fit | Have you actually owned things, and can you tell it clearly | [STAR bank](07-video-interview-and-psychometric.md) |
| **⑥ Bar raiser** | Consistency | Would this person raise or lower the team's average | Everything, plus honesty about failures |

**The reframing that matters:** stages ③ and ④ are **communication tests wearing technical
clothes.** Two candidates with identical knowledge get different outcomes based entirely on how
legibly they think out loud. That is trainable, and almost nobody trains it.

## 3. Loops by company type

The four markets you are applying into run measurably different processes.

### Bangladesh product companies (bKash, Pathao, Shohoz, Brain Station, Therap)

```
  CV screen → technical interview (often 2, back to back) → HR/salary → offer
```

| | |
|---|---|
| Length | 1–3 weeks. Faster than international |
| Style | More knowledge-recall than international; expect direct questions about your stack |
| System design | Present at senior level, often less formal |
| What impresses | Recognisable brands, tenure, breadth, infrastructure knowledge |
| Watch for | Salary asked early and directly. See [the salary question](../resume/03-outreach-and-referrals.md#6-the-salary-question) |

### International remote — product companies (European/US startups and scale-ups)

```
  recruiter → take-home OR technical screen → system design → hiring manager → team fit
```

| | |
|---|---|
| Length | 3–6 weeks |
| Style | Practical over algorithmic. Take-homes common |
| Emphasis | Autonomy, written communication, timezone overlap |
| What impresses | Scale numbers, ownership, clear writing, a real repo |
| Watch for | The written-English signal runs through the whole loop |

### International remote — marketplaces (Toptal, Proxify, Turing, Arc)

```
  application → English/communication screen → heavy technical assessment
             → live technical interview → test project → pool → per-client interviews
```

| | |
|---|---|
| Length | 2–4 weeks to enter the pool, then variable |
| Style | Standardised, high volume, explicit pass rates |
| Emphasis | **English fluency is an explicit, separate gate** |
| What impresses | Passing their bar cleanly; then client-facing communication |
| Watch for | You interview twice — once for the pool, again for each client |

### Large US enterprise (rarer for remote from BD, but happens)

```
  recruiter → OA → phone screen → "onsite": 4-6 rounds including bar raiser → committee
```

| | |
|---|---|
| Length | 6–10 weeks |
| Style | Most algorithmic. LeetCode-style is genuinely required |
| Emphasis | Structured behavioural (Amazon's Leadership Principles style) |
| What impresses | Clean algorithmic execution, well-drilled STAR stories |
| Watch for | The behavioural rounds are weighted far more heavily than candidates expect |

## 4. Where you are most likely to be cut

This repo's own market analysis is blunt about it:

> *"Your funnel risk is concentrated almost entirely in the first two rounds. That is good news —
> early-funnel problems are the most trainable kind."*

**Expected shape of your funnel, before preparation:**

```
   applications         ████████████████████  100
   recruiter screen     ████████               ~20   ← resume quality gates this
   OA / take-home       ███                     ~8   ← THE BIGGEST CUT for your profile
   technical screen     ██                      ~5
   system design        █                       ~3   ← senior vs lead decided here
   final / behavioural  █                       ~2
   offer                ▌                        ~1
```

**Two specific risks named in your profile:**

1. **Online assessment rustiness.** Architect-shaped candidates who have been shipping for six
   years are usually slow on timed algorithmic problems. This is the number one reason people
   with your background never reach a human — and it is the most fixable thing in the list.
2. **Written English artifacts.** For remote roles, the absence of an ADR, a good README or a
   design doc means the hiring manager is guessing. That guess is rarely generous.

**Which implies the prep order:** timed coding practice and the resume first, system design
second, behavioural third. That is exactly how the 30-day plan is weighted, and now you know
why.

## 5. Timelines and running several at once

**Realistic timings, so you can plan rather than worry:**

| Gap | Typical |
|---|---|
| Application → recruiter reply | 3–10 days, or never |
| Recruiter screen → next stage | 3–7 days |
| Take-home issued → deadline | 3–7 days |
| Final loop → decision | 3–10 days |
| Offer → start date | 2–8 weeks |

**Run several in parallel, deliberately.** Not just for volume — **for leverage.** Two offers in
the same fortnight is the single strongest negotiating position available, and it happens by
scheduling rather than by luck.

**How to create that overlap:** apply in batches rather than continuously. Ten applications in
one week produce clustered interviews three weeks later. Applying one per day produces a smear,
and you will never have two live offers at once.

**If one company moves faster than another you prefer**, it is entirely normal to say so:

> "I'm at final stage with another company and expect a decision next week. I'd rather not
> decide before I've completed your process — is there any flexibility in the timeline?"

Most reasonable companies accelerate. The ones that refuse have told you something.

## 6. What happens in the debrief

After the final loop, your interviewers meet and compare notes. Understanding this changes how
you behave in the rounds.

**How the decision is usually made:**

- Each interviewer submits a rating and written evidence **before** hearing others', to reduce
  anchoring.
- They then discuss, and a **single strong objection often sinks a candidate** even when
  everyone else is positive. Consensus hiring is conservative by design.
- The most common blocker at senior level is not a wrong answer — it is *"I couldn't tell what
  they actually did versus what their team did."*

**What this means for you:**

| Implication | Action |
|---|---|
| Someone must **advocate** for you | Give at least one interviewer a memorable, specific story |
| Vague ownership sinks candidates | Say "I" for your work and "we" for the team's, precisely |
| A single unaddressed doubt can end it | Ask the closing question: *"is there anything about my background that gives you hesitation?"* |
| Notes are written, not remembered | Concrete numbers survive the debrief; impressions do not |

**The closing question is your only chance to answer an objection you never heard.** It is in
[the reverse-interview bank](10-reverse-interview-bank.md) and it is the highest-return question
in this repo.

## 7. Questions to ask about the process itself

In the recruiter screen, ask for the map. It is normal, and it lets you prepare precisely.

- What does the process look like end to end, and roughly how long does it take?
- Who would I be interviewing with, and what does each round focus on?
- Is there a take-home or an online assessment? What format and how long?
- Is the system design round more high-level architecture or hands-on?
- Is this a new role or a backfill?
- What is the salary range budgeted for this role?

**Then prepare specifically.** "There's a 90-minute HackerRank with two coding problems and SQL"
is a completely different week of preparation from "a four-hour take-home building a small API."

Most candidates never ask, and then prepare for the wrong thing.

## 8. Reading your own rejections

**Log the stage in the Pipeline tab every time.** Individually a rejection is noise; in
aggregate it is a diagnosis.

| Pattern | Diagnosis | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Never hear back at all | Resume or targeting | [Resume system](../resume/00-resume-system.md), and check role fit |
| Cut after recruiter screen | Level mismatch, comp mismatch, or spoken communication | Rehearse the 90-second intro; ask the range earlier |
| Cut after OA | **Timed coding speed** | [Phase 0](README.md), timed practice, not more reading |
| Cut after technical screen | Thinking out loud, not knowledge | [Live coding](11-live-coding-and-pairing.md), mocks with a human |
| Cut after system design | Framework and structure | [Phase 5](../phase-5-system-design/01-system-design-fundamentals.md), timed drills, recorded |
| Cut after behavioural | Stories lack specifics or numbers | STAR bank; add metrics to every story |
| Reach offer but lose on comp | Negotiating position | Run applications in batches to create overlap |

**Three at the same stage is a pattern and it is fixable. Three spread across stages is just
volume — keep going.**

That distinction matters more than it sounds: the second case is the normal experience of a good
candidate, and mistaking it for the first is how people conclude they are not good enough and
stop in week three — which is exactly when the week-one applications start converting.
