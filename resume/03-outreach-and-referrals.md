# Outreach, Referrals & Follow-ups

> A **referred** application converts several times better than a cold one.
> A cold application **with a short note** converts better than one without.
>
> This document is the scripts. Keep them short — every one is read on a phone, between other
> things.

## The whole document in 60 seconds

1. **Roughly 2–5% of applications become offers.** That is normal, not a judgment on you.
2. **One referral is worth about five cold applications.** The ask costs one message.
3. **Two sentences beats a cover letter.** One number, one reason you read the posting.
4. **Ask for the salary range first.** Whoever names a number first loses.
5. **Never give your current salary.** Especially moving from Bangladesh to an international
   company.
6. **Send a note after every interview** — and use it to improve one answer you gave badly.

## 1. The arithmetic

A strong candidate converts roughly **2–5% of applications into offers.**

That is arithmetic, not a judgment. Understand it now, because it changes how you should feel
in week two.

```
100 cold applications
      ↓  10-20 replies
      ↓   5-8  recruiter screens
      ↓   2-3  final rounds
      ↓   1    offer

 20 referred applications
      ↓   8-12 replies          ← same result, one fifth the applications
      ↓   5-6  screens
      ↓   3    final rounds
      ↓   1    offer
```

**Read those two columns again.** That gap is why this document exists.

**One referral ≈ five cold applications.** And the cost of asking is one message that takes
three minutes to write.

### Two consequences

**1. Thirty applications is a floor, not a goal.** At a 3% conversion rate, 30 applications is
roughly *one* offer. If you want a choice between offers, you need more volume or more
referrals — preferably both.

**2. Your emotional response to any single rejection should be near zero.** The maths says most
will not work. That is the system functioning normally, not you failing.

### Track everything in the Pipeline tab

Not because tracking is virtuous. Because by week three you will genuinely not remember which
of thirty companies you contacted, through whom, when, and what you claimed.

## 2. The two-sentence note

Many application forms have an optional message box. **Fill it.** Two sentences. Never more.

### The formula

```
Sentence 1: the most relevant thing you have done, with the number.
Sentence 2: why THIS role, in concrete terms.
```

### Two examples

> I built and ran the notification platform serving 41M subscribers at [Company] — Kafka-backed,
> 4M+ messages a day, p99 under two seconds through upstream outages. Your posting mentions
> scaling the event pipeline as the immediate problem, which is the part of the job I have
> actually done rather than read about.

> I have spent the last [N] years on event-driven backends at telecom scale, most recently
> moving a 41M-subscriber notification system off a synchronous read path. You are hiring for
> real-time infrastructure, which is where my streaming work at [Company] sits.

### Why these work

Only two things are required, and **most applicants provide neither:**

1. **One verifiable number** — proves you did real work.
2. **One sentence proving you read the posting** — proves you are not mass-mailing.

### What to delete

| Phrase | Why it fails |
|---|---|
| "I am excited about this opportunity" | Everyone writes it. It carries no information |
| "I believe I would be a great fit" | You believe it; that is not evidence |
| "I am passionate about technology" | True of every applicant |
| Anything that survives find-and-replace of the company name | Then it is not about this company |

### Before and after

```
❌ Dear Hiring Manager,

   I am writing to express my strong interest in the Senior Backend Engineer
   position at your esteemed company. I am a passionate developer with 6 years
   of experience and I believe my skills would be a great asset to your team.
   I am a fast learner and a great team player. I look forward to hearing
   from you.
```

That is 60 words and says nothing. Compare:

```
✅ I built and ran the notification platform serving 41M subscribers at
   Banglalink — Kafka-backed, 4M+ messages a day, p99 under two seconds
   through upstream outages. Your posting mentions scaling the event
   pipeline as the immediate problem, which is the part of the job I have
   actually done rather than read about.
```

Roughly the same length. Completely different outcome.

## 3. Asking for a referral

The highest-return message in this document. Three situations.

### 3a. Someone you actually worked with

Be direct, and **make it easy to say no.**

```
Hi [Name] — hope things are good at [Company].

I am looking for senior backend roles at the moment, mostly remote. I saw
[Company] has an opening for [Role] ([link]). Would you be comfortable
referring me? Totally fine if not.

If it helps, here is the short version: [N] years on Node.js/NestJS
event-driven systems, most recently the notification platform serving 41M
subscribers at [Company] — Kafka, 4M+ messages/day. Resume attached.

Either way, good to hear from you.
```

**Why it works:**

| Element | Effect |
|---|---|
| The ask is explicit | They know exactly what you want |
| "Totally fine if not" | Removes the social pressure, which makes yes *easier*, not harder |
| The paste-ready summary | **This is the key part.** They can copy it straight into the referral form |
| Resume attached | No extra step for them |

> **The main reason referrals fail is that the referrer would have to do work.** Their internal
> form asks "why are you recommending this person?" If you have not written that sentence for
> them, they postpone it, and then forget.

### 3b. A loose connection

Someone you met once, or an ex-colleague of an ex-colleague.

**Do not ask for a referral immediately.** Ask for something smaller.

```
Hi [Name] — we overlapped briefly at [context], and I have been following
[specific thing they built or wrote].

I am job-hunting for senior backend roles and [Company] is high on my list.
Rather than ask for a referral outright — would you be up for a fifteen-minute
call about what the backend team is actually working on? If it seems like a
fit after that, I would ask about a referral then.

Context on me: [one line with the number].
```

**Why it works:** you are asking for *information*, not a *favour*. That is much easier to say
yes to.

And in practice, a good number of these calls end with them offering the referral themselves —
because now they have met you and it is no longer a risk to their reputation.

### 3c. Nobody you know at all

**Do not ask a stranger for a referral.** They cannot vouch for you, and asking makes that
obvious.

Message the hiring manager directly instead — see the next section.

## 4. Cold outreach to hiring managers

Skip the recruiter. Message the person who owns the problem.

### Finding them

| Where | What to look for |
|---|---|
| The company's engineering blog | Author names |
| Their GitHub organisation | Who reviews the pull requests |
| Conference talks | Search the company name on YouTube |
| LinkedIn | `Engineering Manager` / `Head of Engineering` / `VP Engineering` at that company |

### LinkedIn connection request (300 character limit)

```
Hi [Name] — I saw the [Role] opening on your team. I have run Kafka-based
event pipelines at 41M-subscriber scale and your posting reads like the
same problem set. Would you be open to a short conversation?
```

### Email or InMail

```
Subject: [Role] — event-driven backend, 41M-subscriber scale

Hi [Name],

I am applying for [Role] and thought it was worth reaching out directly
since you would be the one living with the outcome.

The short version: [N] years on Node.js/NestJS backends, most recently the
notification platform at [Company] serving 41M subscribers — Kafka-backed,
4M+ messages/day, and the circuit-breaker and retry-budget work that kept
p99 under two seconds when providers went down. Before that, real-time
streaming infrastructure on Agora and WebSockets.

Your posting mentions [specific thing]. That is the part I would want to
talk about.

Resume attached; code at github.com/[handle].

Thanks for reading,
[Name] · GMT+6, comfortable overlap with [their timezone]
```

### The rules behind it

| Rule | Reason |
|---|---|
| Put the claim in the subject line | It competes with fifty other subject lines in their inbox |
| One number, early | It is the only thing separating you in the first three seconds |
| Name one specific thing from the posting | Proves this is not a mass email |
| Timezone at the bottom | Answers the objection before it forms |
| Attach only the resume | A portfolio deck will not be opened |

> **"You would be the one living with the outcome"** is doing quiet work in that opening. It
> acknowledges that *they* carry the consequence of the hire — which is exactly how a hiring
> manager thinks, and almost nobody says it.

## 5. Replying to a recruiter

When someone contacts *you*, **reply within a day.** Recruiters work a pipeline and momentum is
real.

### If you are interested

```
Hi [Name] — thanks for reaching out, this looks interesting.

Quick context: [N] years backend, Node.js/NestJS/AWS, most recently
event-driven systems at 41M-subscriber scale. I am open to remote roles and
work GMT+6 with a solid European overlap.

Before we set up a call — could you share the salary range, and whether the
role is genuinely remote or hybrid? Happy to talk either way, it just helps
me prioritise.

Available [two or three specific time slots].
```

**Three things happening here:**
1. You re-established your level and scale in one line.
2. You asked for the range **first** (see [§6](#6-the-salary-question)).
3. You offered specific times — removing a whole round of back-and-forth.

### If you are not interested

```
Hi [Name] — thanks for thinking of me. This one is not the right fit — I am
focused on [senior backend / distributed systems] roles at the moment.

Do keep me in mind if something in that area comes up.
```

**Never ignore a recruiter.** The market you are in is smaller than it looks, and recruiters
change companies constantly. The one you ignore today works somewhere you want in two years.

## 6. The salary question

### Ask for their range first

This is normal, expected, and saves everyone time. It also means **you are not the one who
named a number.**

> **Why naming first is bad:** whoever says a number first sets the anchor. If their budget was
> $90k and you say "$60k would be great", you will be offered $60k. That mistake costs more than
> everything else in this document combined.

### If they push you to name a figure

```
I would rather understand the role properly before talking numbers.
What range has the role been budgeted at?
```

### If they insist

```
Based on my research for [Senior Backend, remote, EU/US company], I am
looking in the [range] band, but that depends a lot on the scope and the
rest of the package. What is the budget for this role?
```

### Two rules that matter more than any script

**1. Never volunteer your current salary.**

For a Bangladesh → international move, this is the single most expensive number you can
disclose.

Why: it anchors your offer to the local market you are **deliberately leaving.** A company that
learns your current salary will offer a "generous" raise on top of it — which may still be far
below what they had budgeted for the role.

> In several US states, asking this is actually illegal. Elsewhere it is simply not in your
> interest to answer.

If asked directly:

```
I would rather not anchor on my current number, since I am moving markets.
I am focused on what the role is worth. What range do you have budgeted?
```

**2. Give a range, never a single number.**

And make the **bottom** of your range a number you would genuinely accept — because that is the
number you will be offered.

```
❌ "I'm looking for around $70,000."
   → You will be offered $70,000, or less.

✅ "I'm looking in the $75,000-90,000 range, depending on scope."
   → You will be offered somewhere around $75,000. Set the floor accordingly.
```

Full negotiation scripts — counter-offers, competing offers, equity — are in the app's
**Playbook** document under Compensation & negotiation.

## 7. Follow-ups, and dealing with silence

### The schedule

| When | What to do |
|---|---|
| Application sent | Log it in Pipeline: date, and which resume version |
| +7 days, no reply | **One** follow-up, if you have a human contact. Otherwise nothing |
| +14 days | Consider it dead. Do **not** send a second follow-up |
| After a screen | Thank-you note within 24 hours |
| After a final round | Thank-you note within 24 hours, then wait for their stated timeline + 3 days |

### The follow-up message

```
Hi [Name] — following up on my application for [Role], sent [date]. Still
very interested; happy to answer anything that would help.

[One new relevant thing, if you have one — a repo you shipped, a related
piece of work.]
```

That last line is optional but valuable. It gives the message a reason to exist beyond "please
notice me."

### About silence

**Most applications end in silence. Including good ones.**

The reasons usually have nothing to do with you:

- The role was filled internally before it was ever really open
- The budget moved or froze
- The hiring manager left
- They received 400 applications and stopped reading at 80

> **This matters more than it sounds.** Treating silence as evidence that you are not good enough
> is both inaccurate and expensive — it is the main reason people stop applying in week three.
>
> Week three is exactly when your week-one applications start converting.

## 8. After the interview

Send within 24 hours. Short. And **use it to do one piece of real work.**

```
Hi [Name],

Thanks for the conversation today — I enjoyed digging into [specific topic].

One thing I want to revisit: you asked about [question], and I said [what you
said]. Thinking about it afterwards, the part I did not mention is [the better
answer]. [Two or three sentences.]

Looking forward to hearing what comes next.
```

### The middle paragraph is the entire point

**Every interview has one question you answered at about 70%.** You knew it as you walked away.

Answering it properly, unprompted, a day later demonstrates exactly the trait a lead role is
screening for: **you keep thinking about the problem after the meeting ends.**

This has flipped outcomes. It is the cheapest advantage available to you.

### A worked example

Suppose they asked *"how would you handle duplicate webhook deliveries?"* and you said "I'd
check if we'd seen the ID before" — correct but shallow.

```
One thing I want to revisit: you asked about duplicate webhook deliveries,
and I said we'd check whether we had seen the ID before. That is the shape
of it, but I skipped the part that actually matters — the check and the
write have to be atomic, or two concurrent deliveries both pass the check
before either writes.

In practice I'd put a unique constraint on the event id and let the database
reject the second insert, rather than doing a read-then-write in application
code. The failed insert is the deduplication.
```

**Do not use this to apologise.** Do not write "sorry, I was nervous." Use it to be better.

## 9. After a rejection

Two sentences, and one question that occasionally pays off.

```
Thanks for letting me know, and for the time your team spent.

If you have any feedback on where I fell short, I would genuinely value it —
and if something closer to my background opens up later, I would be glad to
hear about it.
```

**Roughly one in five will answer with something useful.** That is an excellent return on two
sentences, and their feedback is worth more than another practice problem, because it is
specific to you.

### Log the reason in Pipeline

This turns individual disappointments into a diagnosis:

- **Three rejections at the same stage** = a pattern, and patterns are fixable.
  - All at the online assessment → your DSA speed is the problem
  - All after the system design round → your HLD framework needs work
  - All after the final round → likely the behavioural or communication half
- **Three rejections spread across different stages** = just volume. Keep going.

## 10. Your weekly rhythm

Outreach fails when it is done in bursts of enthusiasm and then not at all. Put it on a clock.

| Day | Action | Time |
|---|---|---|
| **Mon** | Source: find 6–8 new targets, add to Pipeline as wishlist | 45 min |
| **Tue** | Apply to 4, with tailored summary line and two-sentence note | 60 min |
| **Wed** | Two referral or hiring-manager messages | 20 min |
| **Thu** | Apply to 4 | 60 min |
| **Fri** | Follow-ups due this week; update Pipeline stages | 20 min |

**Total: about 3.5 hours a week**, producing roughly 8 applications and 2 warm touches.

That is enough to fill a funnel without eating the study time that makes the interviews go well.

### The one rule

> **Applying is not studying, and studying is not applying.**

Doing only the second one *feels* productive and produces nothing. It is the most common way a
30-day sprint ends with no interviews.

The plan schedules both deliberately, on purpose, for this reason.
